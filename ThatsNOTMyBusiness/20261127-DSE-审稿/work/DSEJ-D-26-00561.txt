Abstract:
This study proposes a Markov Transition Field based Multimodal Sparse Ensemble
(MTF-MSE) method, which reframes multi-step time series forecasting as the
recognition of transition structure. The method operates in three stages. First, a Markov
transition field encodes second-order dynamics between quantised states as a two-
dimensional image. Then, a gated Swin-based vision Transformer fuses these images
with domain-specific textual descriptions to extract multilevel features. Finally, Lasso-
based post-selection inference selects a sparse subset of Bootstrap trees, reducing
forecast variance while enabling valid selective inference. Evaluation covers three
simulations and eight real datasets, thirteen competitors spanning classical, deep, tree-
ensemble and diagnostic configurations, and one- to three-step-ahead MAPE and
MASE. MTF-MSE leads in almost every dataset-horizon cell, and its margin widens
with the horizon. Friedman-Nemenyi and paired tests confirm that the advantage is
significant and stable across independent runs, and the ablation attributes it to the
representation rather than to a stronger learner.

A Markov Transition Field Based Multimodal Sparse Ensemble for Multi-Step Time Series Forecasting 
 
 
Abstract 
This study proposes a Markov Transition Field based Multimodal Sparse Ensemble (MTF-MSE) method, which 
reframes multi-step time series forecasting as the recognition of transition structure. The method operates in three stages. 
First, a Markov transition field encodes second-order dynamics between quantised states as a two-dimensional image. Then, 
a gated Swin-based vision Transformer fuses these images with domain-specific textual descriptions to extract multilevel 
features. Finally, Lasso-based post-selection inference selects a sparse subset of Bootstrap trees, reducing forecast variance 
while enabling valid selective inference. Evaluation covers three simulations and eight real datasets, thirteen competitors 
spanning classical, deep, tree-ensemble and diagnostic configurations, and one- to three-step-ahead MAPE and MASE. 
MTF-MSE leads in almost every dataset-horizon cell, and its margin widens with the horizon. Friedman-Nemenyi and 
paired tests confirm that the advantage is significant and stable across independent runs, and the ablation attributes it to the 
representation rather than to a stronger learner. 
Keywords: multi-step time series forecasting; Markov transition field; transition structure; image-based representation; 
multimodal feature extraction; sparse post-selection inference 
1 Introduction 
A time series is a sequence of observations collected in chronological order, and such data arise broadly across 
scientific and engineering disciplines including meteorology, finance, medicine, and energy systems, recording the dynamic 
trajectory of system states as they evolve over time[1–5]. These data typically exhibit complex characteristics such as trend, 
seasonality, and stochastic fluctuations. The process of modeling and analyzing time series data, with the aim of uncovering 
underlying patterns from historical observations and thereby producing reliable forecasts of future states, constitutes the 
field of time series forecasting. 
Numerous classical methods have been developed to address the time series forecasting problem. Since its introduction, 
the Box-Jenkins methodology has established the statistical foundation of this field[6]. Its central model, ARIMA, combines 
autoregressive, differencing, and moving average components, expressing them in compact mathematical form through the 
lag operator, and provides a rigorous, systematic three-stage modeling procedure comprising model identification, 
parameter estimation, and diagnostic checking[7]. By emphasizing stationarity transformation and residual white noise 
testing, the methodology ensures model robustness and interpretability, and has exerted a lasting influence on subsequent 
research. Building on this foundation, the seasonal ARIMA (SARIMA) model was developed to accommodate data with 
periodic fluctuations; by incorporating seasonal differencing, it effectively captures the cyclical regularities present in the 
series[8]. As a benchmark against which the performance of these more sophisticated models is commonly assessed, the 
random walk with drift (RW-DRIFT) model characterizes the series as the superposition of a deterministic trend and 
stochastic shocks, and is widely employed to evaluate the relative forecasting gains achieved by more complex 
specifications[9]. Nevertheless, all of these models rest on linearity assumptions, which renders them ill-suited to capturing 
the nonlinear relationships and structural breaks that are pervasive in real-world data; their sensitivity to outliers constitutes 
a further limitation. These shortcomings have motivated the research community to explore nonlinear and time-varying 
modeling approaches. 
As a representative nonlinear method, the threshold autoregressive (TAR) model partitions the state space through 
threshold functions and estimates parameters separately within each regime, providing a theoretical framework for 
modeling asymmetric responses such as financial volatility and business cycles[10–13]. Subsequent work combined classical 
statistical models with artificial neural networks to produce hybrid architectures, including the feedforward neural network 
autoregressive (NNET-AR) model and SARIMA-NN. Such hybrid models retain the capacity of linear models to 
decompose deterministic trends and seasonal components while employing neural networks to fit the nonlinear patterns 
remaining in the residuals, achieving complementary strengths, and in numerous complex scenarios, attaining forecasting 
performance superior to either constituent approach alone[14–17]. However, hybrid models entail high design complexity and 
require adequate historical samples for parameter estimation, which limits their applicability in data-scarce settings or

applications with stringent real-time requirements. To address small-sample modeling needs, grey models offer an 
alternative pathway. These models do not require a large volume of historical observations; instead, they apply the 
accumulated generating operation (AGO) to the raw data in order to uncover latent systemic regularities, conferring a 
distinctive advantage in contexts characterized by data insufficiency or high uncertainty. Grey models thus constitute an 
important complement to the broader toolkit of time series forecasting methods[18–21]. 
Beyond traditional statistical methods and hybrid models, deep learning approaches have been extensively explored in 
the domain of time series forecasting. Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) introduced deep learning to this domain[22], while 
capable of retaining temporal information, they suffer from the vanishing and exploding gradient problems, which impairs 
their ability to model long-range dependencies[23]. Long short-term memory (LSTM) networks alleviate this deficiency 
through gating mechanisms and have demonstrated strong performance on univariate stationary series; however, their 
inherently sequential computation results in reduced efficiency when processing long sequences[24]. Temporal convolutional 
networks (TCNs) address this limitation by introducing dilated convolution and causal convolution, improving efficiency 
through parallel computation and a controllable receptive field, yet they remain constrained in capturing global 
dependencies[25]. The Transformer architecture overcomes this constraint through the self-attention mechanism, enabling 
parallel modeling of dependencies between arbitrary time steps within a sequence and substantially enhancing long-range 
forecasting capacity; however, its quadratic complexity poses significant memory and computational challenges when 
applied to very long sequences[26–28]. To address this challenge, subsequent studies proposed various sparse attention 
mechanisms that reduce complexity to sub-quadratic or log-linear levels, achieving improved performance on long-horizon 
forecasting tasks[29–32]. More recently, the integration of time-domain and frequency-domain methods has further broadened 
the modeling perspective; by decomposing sequences into frequency components via the Fourier transform and dynamically 
combining them, accuracy gains have been achieved on certain datasets, although adaptability to non-stationary signals 
remains an unresolved challenge[33, 34]. 
In summary, time series forecasting methods have undergone continuous evolution, progressing from classical 
statistical models to nonlinear approaches, hybrid architectures, and ultimately deep learning. Classical ARIMA-class 
models established the theoretical foundation through a rigorous systematic framework, yet they are constrained by linearity 
assumptions; nonlinear methods and hybrid models partially overcome this constraint but face challenges of sample 
dependency and design complexity; while deep learning methods have demonstrated considerable capacity in modeling 
long-range dependencies and capturing complex patterns, their interpretability and the specificity of their feature selection 
remain areas that warrant further improvement. Against this backdrop, how to effectively integrate multi-perspective 
representations of time series data, while maintaining forecasting accuracy and simultaneously enhancing model 
interpretability and inferential capacity, constitutes an important direction for further inquiry. To this end, this paper 
proposes a time series forecasting method that integrates image-based representation with sparse inference. First, the 
Markov transition field is employed to encode one-dimensional time series as two-dimensional images, thereby preserving 
the temporal dynamic structure. An optimized deep learning model is then applied to extract latent semantic features from 
the resulting images. Finally, the extracted features are fed into a Lasso-based model for feature selection and inferential 
forecasting. Through a multi-stage framework combining image-based reconstruction, deep feature extraction, and sparse 
inference, the proposed method aims to integrate the representational strengths of deep learning with the interpretability of 
classical statistical models, offering a novel approach to time series forecasting. 
2 Methodology 
This section proposes a Markov Transition Field based Multimodal Sparse Ensemble (MTF-MSE) method for time 
series forecasting. The method comprises three core stages, which are introduced in sequence in Sections 2.1 through 2.3. 
2.1 Time Series to Image Transformation 
In the first stage, the proposed model employs the Markov transition field (MTF) to convert time series data into 
two-dimensional images. The Markov transition field is a stochastic process model based on Markov chains, primarily used 
to characterize and analyze the probabilistic transition relationships between states in time series data. In the MTF 
framework, the time series data are partitioned into a set of discrete cells or states, and a Markov chain is used to model the 
transitions between states within each cell. The main steps by which the proposed model converts time series data into a

(1) The k-means clustering algorithm is applied to partition the time series data into a series of discrete state intervals, 
and a unique code is assigned to each state. 
(2) A state transition matrix is initialized according to the number of states. The matrix has dimensions equal to the 
square of the number of states, that is, given N states, the matrix is of size N×N. 
(3) The time series data are traversed and, for each pair of consecutive time steps, the number of transitions from one 
state to another is recorded. These counts are then used to compute the state transition probabilities, which are filled into the 
state transition matrix; the entries in each row are constrained to sum to one so as to satisfy the requirements of a valid 
probability distribution. 
(4) The state transition matrix is converted into an image, in which each pixel represents the transition probability from 
one state to another, with pixel intensity encoding the magnitude of the probability. 
Figure 1 presents an example of converting time series data into an image using the proposed model, where the left 
panel shows the original data and the right panel shows the resulting two-dimensional image. The main diagonal of the 
image represents the self-persistence of states at each time point. The alternating light-and-dark texture near the diagonal 
encodes the short-term state transition patterns of the series, while the overall distribution and structure of the image texture 
reflect the long-term periodicity and trend inherent in the time series.

2.2 Feature Extraction 
This study proposes a novel approach to image feature extraction that integrates a Vision Transformer, attention 
mechanisms, and multimodal fusion. The overall process proceeds in four sub-steps: 
(1) The system receives an input image. It assesses the image dimensions and, if the image is too large, uniformly 
crops it to a standardized 256×256 format to ensure consistent input dimensions for all subsequent processing. 
(2) The preprocessed image is passed into the feature extraction module. The core of this module is a model named 
Ctran, built on the Swin-Tiny Transformer architecture and incorporating a Contmix augmentation module. The model first 
encodes the image through a three-layer convolutional stem (ConvStem), then captures multi-level features via the Swin 
Transformer, and finally refines the feature representations using the Contmix module in conjunction with attention 
mechanisms and positional encoding techniques, outputting one or more high-dimensional feature vectors for each image. 
(3) If a single image yields multiple feature vectors in the preceding stage (for example, from different cropped 
regions), the system concatenates these vectors to form a two-dimensional feature matrix of shape (n, 768), where n denotes 
the number of feature vectors. 
(4) The concatenated visual feature matrix enters the aggregation module. Here, the system incorporates not only the 
image features but also textual descriptive information (encoded as a vector) pertaining to the domain of the original time 
series. A gating mechanism is employed to dynamically weight each image feature vector, accentuating salient components 
while suppressing noise. The weighted image features are subsequently fused with the text features to form a unified 
multimodal representation.

2.3 Model Prediction 
The feature-extracted data are divided into a training set and a test set. Bootstrap sampling is applied to draw M sample 
subsets from the training set, and one decision tree (referred to as a constituent tree) is constructed on each subset, yielding 
M constituent trees in total. 
All M constituent trees are used to generate predictions on the test set, producing an M-dimensional prediction matrix. 
The mean prediction is taken as the response variable and the predictions of the individual constituent trees serve as the 
features for Lasso regression fitting, through which L1 regularization shrinks the coefficients of a subset of constituent trees 
to zero. The formulation is as follows:

2.4 Summary 
The MTF-MSE method proposed in this section achieves time series forecasting through the coordinated operation of 
three stages. First, the Markov transition field is employed to convert one-dimensional time series into two-dimensional 
images, explicitly encoding the probabilistic transition structure between states. Second, the Ctran Vision Transformer and a 
gating fusion mechanism are combined to extract multi-level features from the converted images, which are then integrated 
with domain-specific textual descriptions through multimodal fusion. Third, multiple decision trees are constructed via 
Bootstrap sampling, and Lasso regularization is applied to dynamically select the optimal subset of constituent models for 
ensemble forecasting. By preserving the structural information of the time series data, the method enhances forecasting 
accuracy and robustness through multimodal complementary augmentation and a sparse ensemble strategy. The complete 
processing pipeline of the proposed method is illustrated in Figure 3. 
 
Figure 3. Framework of the MTF-MSE method 
It should be noted that the design of MTF-MSE is not a stack of off-the-shelf components, each stage is introduced to 
remedy a specific deficiency that the preceding stage cannot resolve. (i) In the temporal domain, discriminative structure is 
largely carried by second-order transition dynamics between quantised states, to which lag-based models have only implicit 
access. The Markov transition field makes this structure explicit by re-organising the series as a two-dimensional field of 
transition probabilities indexed by both time step and state. (ii) The resulting image encodes multi-scale textures, diagonal 
state persistence, near-diagonal short-term transitions, and off-diagonal long-range periodicity, whose joint interpretation 
requires a global, content-adaptive receptive field rather than the fixed local kernels of a convolutional network. We 
therefore adopt an optimized Swin-based vision Transformer. (iii) A purely visual representation is agnostic to the semantics 
of the underlying domain, a gated fusion with domain-specific textual descriptions injects this missing semantic prior. (iv) 
Deep features obtained in this way are high-dimensional and strongly collinear, and feeding them directly into a tree 
ensemble inflates the variance of the forecast. We therefore replace the conventional averaging step of bagging with a 
Lasso-based post-selection inference procedure, which performs sparsity-driven model selection, variance reduction and

3 Simulation Study 
In this section, the performance of MTF-MSE on simulated data is evaluated. In each simulation, 1,000 time series of 
length 300 are generated; 700 of these are randomly selected to form the training set and the remaining 300 constitute the 
test set. MTF-MSE is compared against a broad set of established forecasting methods on the generated datasets, all sharing 
the same 700/300 training/test partition, the same horizon set H = {1, 2, 3}, and an equal hyper-parameter budget tuned on a 
held-out validation split, so that the comparison is not confounded by unequal tuning effort. All reported figures are means 
over R = 100 Monte Carlo replications. 
The comparison begins with three classical baselines: the autoregressive integrated moving average model (ARIMA), a 
feedforward neural network autoregressive model (NNET-AR), and the long short-term memory recurrent neural network 
(LSTM), and is extended with nine further models grouped into two families plus one diagnostic. The first family comprises 
five deep time-series models, PatchTST, TimesNet, DLinear, N-BEATS, and Informer. They are trained under their 
canonical architectures with the Adam optimiser and early stopping (patience of 10 epochs), using a look-back window of L 
= 300 identical to that of MTF-MSE. The second family comprises four tree ensembles applied directly to the raw series: 
Random Forest, ExtraTrees, XGBoost, and LightGBM, each trained on a lag matrix built from the L = 300 most recent 
observations together with calendar and seasonal indicators, with no image-based encoding whatsoever. This family makes 
it possible to assess whether the image-based representation is necessary at all, i.e., whether a strong tabular learner 
operating on lagged raw values already attains comparable accuracy. As a diagnostic we further report GBDT + flattened 
MTF, which feeds the flattened transition-field vector to the same gradient-boosted tree learner so as to separate the 
contribution of the representation from that of the learner: the tree ensembles (500 trees for Random Forest and ExtraTrees; 
2,000 boosting rounds with early stopping for XGBoost and LightGBM; maximum depth 6; learning rate 0.05) use no 
information from the transition field, whereas the diagnostic uses all of it but discards its two-dimensional organisation.

4.1 Experimental Results 
The MAPE values of the fourteen methods on the eight real datasets listed in Table 9 are reported in Table 10, and the 
following conclusions can be drawn. First, MTF-MSE attains the lowest MAPE in twenty-three of the twenty-four 
dataset-horizon combinations, and its lead over the closest competitor widens steadily as the forecasting step extends, the 
mean relative error reduction over the best rival rises from about 4.3% at one step to about 8.1% at two steps and about 10.0% 
at three steps. The smooth CSI 500 index remains below 0.05 for MTF-MSE at every horizon, whereas the three-step-ahead 
MAPE reaches 0.436174 on the irregular weekly ILI series and 0.676739 at two steps on the volatile Beijing PM2.5 
concentration, so the heterogeneity in domain, sampling frequency, and volatility recorded in Table 9 is faithfully reflected 
in the results. Second, the families of methods separate in a stable and interpretable way. Within the deep time-series model 
family PatchTST is consistently the strongest and Informer the second strongest, while TimesNet, DLinear, and N-BEATS 
exchange the remaining positions across datasets and horizons according to their differing sensitivity to periodicity and 
sampling frequency. Among the raw-sequence tree ensembles LightGBM is generally the best and ExtraTrees and Random 
Forest the weakest, and the classical baselines deteriorate most sharply on the seasonal and volatile series, ARIMA rises to 
1.108505 at three steps on the Shandong township temperature data, more than seven times the 0.152925 of MTF-MSE, 
whereas LSTM is marginally ahead of MTF-MSE only at one step on the Beijing PM2.5 concentration (0.164596 against 
0.174521) and loses that advantage as soon as the horizon lengthens. Finally, the closest competitor is most often PatchTST, 
followed by the GBDT + flattened MTF diagnostic, and the latter never falls below MTF-MSE on any dataset or horizon 
even though it receives the same transition-field information. Since the diagnostic discards only the two-dimensional 
organisation of the field, its intermediate position reproduces on real data the separation between the representation effect 
and the spatial-encoding effect documented in Section 3, and the fact that the advantage of MTF-MSE grows with the 
horizon on hourly, 15-minute, and weekly series alike indicates that the gain originates in the Markov transition field 
making second-order transition dynamics explicit rather than in the choice of base learner. In summary, MTF-MSE is not 
merely the most accurate method on average but the one whose accuracy is most robust to both the regime of the series and 
the length of the forecast.

Table 11 records the MASE values of fourteen methods on eight datasets, and the following conclusions can be drawn 
from the table. First, the overall pattern is consistent with the MAPE results, MTF-MSE maintains the lowest MASE in 
twenty-three of the twenty-four combinations and stays below the naïve forecast benchmark of 1 in every one of them, with 
values ranging from 0.021352 on the CSI 500 index to 0.869463 on the Beijing PM2.5 concentration. Its lead over the best 
rival again widens with the forecasting step, from about 4.3% at one step to about 7.6% at two steps and about 9.2% at three 
steps. Because MASE normalises the forecast error by the in-sample error of the naïve forecast, it also makes the influence 
of data structure more transparent, on the CSI 500 index, Electricity (ECL), and Traffic series every method remains below 
1 at all three horizons, whereas on the Shandong township temperature, Beijing PM2.5 concentration, ETTm2, and ILI 
series a number of classical and tree baselines exceed 1 at the two-step and three-step horizons. Second, the individual 
methods differ in a way that mirrors their behaviour on MAPE. ARIMA and NNET-AR are the first to cross the benchmark, 
ARIMA reaches 1.212067 at three steps on the Shandong township temperature data and 1.461588 on ILI, while LSTM 
stays below 1 on most datasets but is overtaken by PatchTST and by the GBDT + flattened MTF diagnostic as the horizon 
grows. Within the deep time-series model family PatchTST again ranks first and Informer second on every dataset and 
horizon, and the closest competitor to MTF-MSE is once more most often PatchTST, followed by the diagnostic and 
LightGBM, so no single rival family accounts for the remaining gap. Finally, taken together with Table 10, the MASE 
results reinforce the central argument of the paper. A value below 1 indicates that a model has extracted genuine predictive 
structure rather than merely fitted the scale of the series, and MTF-MSE, PatchTST, and the GBDT + flattened MTF 
diagnostic are the only methods that remain below 1 in all twenty-four cells, with MTF-MSE recording the lowest value in 
twenty-three of them. The distance of MTF-MSE to the benchmark stays comparatively stable on the irregular ILI and 
ETTm2 series, where most baselines deteriorate sharply, and the single one-step exception on the Beijing PM2.5 
concentration echoes the one-step exception of Simulation 3 discussed in Section 3, in both cases the shortest horizon leaves 
too little scope for the transition-field representation to assert itself and the advantage disappears once multi-step 
dependence matters. This horizon-robust and regime-robust behaviour is consistent with the ablation result of Section 4.4, 
where removing the MTF encoding causes the largest degradation in multi-step accuracy. In summary, encoding 
second-order temporal dependence at the representation level and stabilising it through sparse post-selection inference is 
what allows MTF-MSE to combine accuracy with error stability across the heterogeneous real datasets considered in this 
section.

In summary, what separates the methods in these figures is the slope of error accumulation rather than the error level at 
any single horizon. Encoding second-order temporal dependence at the representation level turns point-by-point 
extrapolation into the recognition of transition structure and interrupts the compounding of multi-step error, which is why 
the advantage is both regime-robust and horizon-robust and widens as the forecast extends.

To attach an interval to the conclusions of Section 4.1 rather than to individual forecasts, the 48 dataset–horizon–metric

two-sided 95% confidence intervals are formed from the t distribution with (n - 1) degrees of freedom. As shown in Figure

n = 48), and the corresponding reduction over ARIMA is 33.84% (95% CI [28.06%, 39.62%]). Split by horizon, the 
advantage over the best rival rises from 4.28% (95% CI [0.39%, 8.17%]) at h = 1 to 8.14% ([6.38%, 9.91%]) at h = 2 and 
10.03% ([8.01%, 12.05%]) at h = 3 for MAPE, and from 4.26% ([1.32%, 7.20%]) to 7.62% ([4.74%, 10.51%]) and 9.16% 
([6.62%, 11.69%]) for MASE. Every lower bound is positive, so the advantage is not an artefact of a particular dataset or 
metric, while the visibly wider interval at one step reflects the one-step exception on the Beijing PM2.5 concentration 
identified in Section 4.1.

4.4 Component Contributions 
Table 12 reports the ablation study, each variant is run R = 100 times and compared with the full configuration by the 
Diebold-Mariano test. Removing the MTF encoding (V1) degrades three-step-ahead MAPE by 34.2% on average 
(Diebold-Mariano test: p = 0.003), which indicates that the image-based representation, rather than the downstream learner, 
is the principal source of the multi-step advantage. Replacing the Swin backbone with a convolutional encoder (V2) reduces 
the gain by 12.7%, confirming that a global receptive field is needed to read the multi-scale texture of the transition field. 
Removing the gated textual branch (V3) changes aggregate MAPE by 8.6% ( p = 0.031), which is consistent with its 
intended role as a semantic prior. Replacing the sparse selection step with plain averaging (V4) does not change accuracy 
significantly ( p = 0.158) but raises the s.d. by 27%, indicating that Lasso contributes mainly to variance reduction rather 
than to accuracy. Substituting ridge for the lasso penalty (V5) yields an intermediate result, so sparsity, and not merely 
regularization, is responsible for this effect. Across the R repetitions, the selected subsets agree with one another at an 
average Jaccard index of 0.86, and 90% of the M trees are selected in more than 80% of runs, indicating that the selected 
subset is stable rather than an artefact of a particular Bootstrap draw.

This study reframes multi-step forecasting as the recognition of transition structure rather than the extrapolation of 
individual observations. Encoding second-order transition dynamics between quantised states in a Markov transition field, 
and coupling the resulting image-based representation with multimodal feature extraction and sparse post-selection 
inference, allows accuracy, interpretability and inferential capacity to be pursued within one procedure. The central finding 
is that the gain originates in the representation rather than in a stronger learner, so it is regime-robust and horizon-robust, 
remains stable across independent runs, and strengthens as the forecast extends. Its boundary is equally informative, 
because the marginal value of the encoding is smallest on short, weakly structured series and cannot be read as uniform 
superiority. MTF-MSE therefore establishes a domain of applicability in which an explicit transition-field representation, 
rather than the choice of downstream model, determines how multi-step error propagates. 
At a more general level, treating a series as a field of state transitions shifts where the modeling burden falls, because 
long-range dependence is carried by the representation rather than accumulated by the learner. The resulting robustness 
depends on a partition into quantised states that is settled outside the fitted model and delimits which dependencies can be 
observed at all. Interpretability is likewise structural rather than causal, and post-selection inference remains conditional on 
the Bootstrap sampling and Lasso penalty that define the ensemble. These qualifications bound what the empirical claim 
licenses without altering it. 
The same qualifications indicate where the approach can be developed further. Adaptive state partitioning and 
multivariate transition fields would show whether the representation scales beyond univariate settings, while probabilistic 
forecasting and calibrated prediction intervals would extend the sparse ensemble to risk assessment. Combining 
post-selection inference with more efficient sequence architectures, and checking the validity of the resulting intervals under 
repeated Bootstrap selection, would determine whether the balance between accuracy, interpretability and computational 
cost survives on larger and more heterogeneous data.

A Markov Transition Field Based Multimodal Sparse Ensemble for Multi-Step 
Time Series Forecasting 
 
Hui Wang* 
* Corresponding author, PhD, Email address: wh_data007@zufe.edu.cn, School of 
Data Sciences, Zhejiang University of Finance and Economics, Hangzhou 310018, 
P.R. China. 
Abstract 
This study proposes a Markov Transition Field based Multimodal Sparse Ensemble 
(MTF-MSE) method, which reframes multi-step time series forecasting as the recognition of 
transition structure. The method operates in three stages. First, a Markov transition field encodes 
second-order dynamics between quantised states as a two-dimensional image. Then, a gated 
Swin-based vision Transformer fuses these images with domain-specific textual descriptions to 
extract multilevel features. Finally, Lasso-based post-selection inference selects a sparse subset of 
Bootstrap trees, reducing forecast variance while enabling valid selective inference. Evaluation 
covers three simulations and eight real datasets, thirteen competitors spanning classical, deep, 
tree-ensemble and diagnostic configurations, and one- to three-step-ahead MAPE and MASE. 
MTF-MSE leads in almost every dataset-horizon cell, and its margin widens with the horizon. 
Friedman-Nemenyi and paired tests confirm that the advantage is significant and stable across 
independent runs, and the ablation attributes it to the representation rather than to a stronger 
learner. 
Keywords: multi-step time series forecasting; Markov transition field; transition structure; 
image-based representation; multimodal feature extraction; sparse post-selection inference
