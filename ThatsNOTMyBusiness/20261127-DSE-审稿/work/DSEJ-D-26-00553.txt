Abstract:
Weight-sharing neural architecture search (NAS) accelerates convolutional neural
network design via an over-parameterized supernet. However, gradient interference
degrades candidate accuracy when topology search, structured channel pruning, and
knowledge distillation are executed sequentially. We propose EvoMentor-Net, a tri-
level co-evolutionary framework that jointly optimizes macro-skeleton depth, micro-cell
directed acyclic graph (DAG) connectivity with channel pruning masks, and a dynamic
population-level mentorship graph within a single supernet. Level-1 genomes encode
active stages and node capacities; Level-2 genomes specify per-stage adjacency
matrices, inverted residual operators, and binary pruning masks; and Level-3 genomes
govern a directed mentorship network with adaptive temperature and loss weights.
Optimized via Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-II), the Level-3
mentorship transitions at 40% of the generational budget from diverse exploration to
hierarchical refinement, restricting distillation to accuracy-verified Pareto-optimal
teachers. Evaluated on CIFAR-10 and the 9-class PathMNIST colon pathology
benchmark on an NVIDIA T4 GPU, EvoMentor-Net achieves 80.18% validation
accuracy on CIFAR-10 with 55.80MFLOPs and 224.15 K parameters, outperforming
Once-for-All NAS (71.16% at 71.63MFLOPs) and bi-level pruning NAS (63.84%).
Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests across five seeds confirm statistically significant
gains (W = 15.00, p = 0.0312) of +10.10% to +16.45% over baselines. Curriculum
mentorship yields a +7.69% accuracy gain over unmentored search, crossdomain
transfer reaches 85.90% accuracy, and direct medical evolution achieves 80.30%
accuracy, 97.54% specificity, and 0.9741 ROC-AUC.

1. Introduction
Deploying deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs)
on resource-constrained clinical workstations and embedded
edge processors requires balancing predictive accuracy against
floating-point operations (FLOPs) and parameter memory foot-
print [1, 2, 3]. In automated histopathology grading, for in-
stance, a diagnostic classifier must separate subtle morphologi-
cal tissue patterns across multiple disease categories while op-
erating within strict hardware budgets on commodity graphics
processing units (GPUs) [4, 5]. Conventionally, designing such
compact models relies on a sequential three-stage pipeline: an
engineer first executes neural architecture search (NAS) to iden-
tify a candidate backbone [6, 7], subsequently applies struc-
tured channel pruning to remove redundant filters [8, 9], and
finally retrains the compressed student network via knowledge
distillation (KD) from a fixed, pre-trained high-capacity teacher
[10, 11].
One may ask why this decoupled three-stage paradigm re-
mains suboptimal when applied to modern weight-sharing su-
pernets such as Once-for-All (OFA) [12] or single-path one-
shot NAS [13]. The answer lies in the structural coupling be-
tween network topology, channel width, and gradient flow dur-
ing weight-sharing training. First, an architecture that maxi-
mizes validation accuracy at full channel width often suffers se-
vere representation degradation when 25% to 75% of its chan-
nels are pruned post hoc, because inter-layer feature dependen-
cies were never regularized to tolerate sparse channel masks
during architectural search [14, 15]. Second, when a popula-
tion of heterogeneous sub-networks shares a single set of con-
volutional weights in a supernet, gradients from small, heavily
pruned sub-networks conflict with gradients from deep, wide
sub-networks [16, 17]. Third, attempting to stabilize super-
net training via static self-distillation, where a fixed set of sub-
networks teaches the remainder of the population [18, 19], fails
to account for evolutionary non-stationarity: a sub-network ini-
tialized as a teacher in generation 1 may be outperformed by
mutated offspring in generation 3, causing static distillation
edges to propagate inaccurate logit distributions and induce
negative transfer.
Why not simply use a bi-level evolutionary search over ar-
chitecture and pruning ratios without inter-subnet mentorship,
or assign fixed top-K individuals as permanent teachers? In
our ablation experiments on CIFAR-10, static top-K mentor-
ship degrades validation accuracy by 3.79 percentage points
compared to unmentored training (dropping from 71.16% to
67.37%), even though it reaches 85% of its lower peak accu-
racy one generation earlier. Why does this happen? When fixed
teacher sub-networks are selected before the population has
converged, their soft targets reinforce early local minima and

higher-accuracy students. During the remaining 60% of gen-

ity is restricted to the top 50% most accurate elite individuals
within the Rank-0 front, concentrating high-precision soft tar-
gets onto promising offspring prior to standalone sub-network
fine-tuning.

2. Related Work
2.1. Evolutionary Neural Architecture Search and Supernets
Early neural architecture search formulations treated network
topology design as a discrete reinforcement learning problem
[6, 21] or a population-based evolutionary search [22, 23], train-
ing thousands of candidate networks from scratch at thousands
of GPU-days. To make architecture search feasible on single-
GPU systems, Pham et al. [24] introduced parameter sharing
among child models in ENAS, while Liu et al. [7] relaxed the
discrete operator choices into continuous architectural weights
optimized via bi-level gradient descent in DARTS. However,
continuous relaxation suffers from performance collapse dur-
ing final discretization and requires memory proportional to the
number of candidate operations [25, 26].
One-shot
weight-sharing
supernets
decoupled
supernet
weight optimization from evolutionary search [16, 13].
Cai
et al. [12] proposed Once-for-All (OFA), which progressively
shrinks kernel sizes, depth, and expansion widths inside a
MobileNetV2-style supernet [2], whereas Lu et al. [27] cou-
pled NSGA-II [20] with evolutionary search in NSGA-Net to
approximate the Pareto frontier between accuracy and com-
putational complexity. While OFA and evolutionary one-shot
methods reduce search cost, they train sampled sub-networks
either independently via uniform path sampling [13] or under
the static supervision of the single largest supernet configura-
tion [12, 17]. In contrast, EvoMentor-Net replaces fixed full-
network supervision with an evolving population of peer sub-
networks whose macro-depth, intra-stage DAG connections,
channel masks, and mentorship topology co-adapt simultane-
ously.
2.2. Structured Channel Pruning and Joint Compression
Structured channel pruning removes entire convolutional fil-
ters to achieve hardware acceleration on general-purpose pro-
cessors without specialized sparse matrix libraries [8, 9, 28].
Classical pruning pipelines rank filters by ℓ1-norm magnitude
[8], Batch Normalization scaling parameters [29], or geometric
median redundancy [30] on a fixed hand-crafted architecture.
However, Liu et al. [14] showed that the pruned channel con-
figuration itself, rather than the inherited weights alone, dictates
final efficiency, motivating the integration of channel pruning
into architecture search [31, 32].
Bi-level methods such as TAS [31] and APQ [32] jointly
search layer widths or quantization bit-widths alongside macro-
architecture. Nevertheless, when binary channel masks evolve
concurrently with irregular intra-stage DAG topologies inside
a shared supernet, aggressive channel masking in early gen-
erations perturbs Batch Normalization running statistics and
starves downstream nodes of gradient signal. EvoMentor-Net
addresses this instability by embedding binary channel masks
m(s) directly into the Level-2 genome with damped mutation
rates (0.5µ), executing 4-batch Batch Normalization recalibra-
tion prior to every fitness evaluation, and compensating for
pruned capacity via multi-mentor knowledge distillation.
2.3. Self-Distillation and Collaborative Evolutionary Learning
Knowledge distillation transfers softened class probability
distributions from a high-capacity teacher to a compact stu-
dent [10, 33]. Moving beyond single static teachers, Zhang et
al. [34] introduced Deep Mutual Learning (DML), demonstrat-
ing that a cohort of peer networks trained collaboratively via
pairwise Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence outperforms iso-
lated training. Subsequent studies investigated born-again net-
works [35], online ensemble distillation [36], and layer-wise
self-distillation [19].
Despite these advances, applying collaborative distillation to
a population of evolving sub-networks introduces a graph topol-
ogy optimization problem. If all Npop individuals distill knowl-
edge to one another via a complete graph, distillation complex-
ity scales quadratically as O(N2
pop) and poorly performing muta-
tions corrupt elite representations. Conversely, if a static subset
of top-ranked individuals is fixed as permanent teachers [18],
premature convergence occurs. EvoMentor-Net resolves this
by encoding the distillation topology as an evolvable Level-3
genome constrained by maximum student in-degree (Kmax = 2),
graph sparsity targets, and a curriculum phase transition that
adapts teacher selection to the maturity of the Pareto frontier.

3.5. Training Procedure and Algorithmic Complexity
Algorithm 1 outlines the complete tri-level co-evolutionary
execution of EvoMentor-Net.
During inner-loop supernet training, evaluating all Npop =
10 sub-networks and their teachers on every mini-batch would
increase forward-pass computation tenfold. Instead, we sample
Ksample = 3 student sub-networks per batch and maintain a per-
batch dictionary cache C of detached teacher logits. Because
at most Kmax = 2 elite mentors are assigned to each student
and multiple students frequently share the same Rank-0 mentor,
caching eliminates redundant teacher evaluations.
Before computing validation accuracy for any parent or off-
spring sub-network, the supernet is placed in train() mode
with gradients disabled and executes 4 forward passes on
pre-fetched training mini-batches to recalibrate the running
mean and variance of all Batch Normalization layers along
that specific sub-network path. Following G generations, the
top-performing candidate I∗undergoes standalone fine-tuning
(finetune_subnet) for Eft = 6 epochs using stochastic gra-
dient descent (SGD, lr = 0.02, momentum 0.9, weight decay
10−4, cosine annealing) restricted strictly to the active parame-
ters of (g∗

for Npop = 10. Analytical FLOPs and parameter evaluation

Rank-0 teachers during inner-loop training, preserving inter-
class decision boundaries even when channels are masked.

4.3. Ablation Studies and Co-Evolutionary Dynamics (E2 &
E7)
To isolate the contribution of the Level-3 curriculum men-
torship graph, Experiment 2 (Table 4) and Experiment 7 (Table
5 and Fig. 4) evaluate three mentorship regimes over G = 5
generations: (1) No Mentorship (E = ∅, pure cross-entropy
loss), (2) Static Top-K Mentorship (indices 0 and 1 perma-
nently teach indices 2 . . . 9 with fixed τ = 4.0, λ = 0.3), and (3)
EvoMentor-Net Evolved Level-3 Mentorship.
Three key findings emerge from Tables 4 and 5:
1. Negative Transfer in Static Mentorship: Static Top-K
mentorship reduces final accuracy from 71.16% (No Men-
torship) to 67.37% (-3.79%). Because individuals 0 and 1
are randomly initialized at Generation 0 without topologi-
cal superiority, locking them as permanent teachers forces
all other sub-networks to mimic arbitrary early logit distri-
butions, homogenizing the population and degrading the
final Pareto frontier.
2. Dynamic Edge Pruning During Mid-Evolution:
In-
specting Table 5 reveals an important self-regulating be-
havior in EvoMentor-Net: active mentorship edges drop
from 6 in Generation 1 to 0 in Generations 2, 3, and 4,
before re-forming 2 elite edges in Generation 5. One may
ask why the mentorship graph temporarily zeroes out its
edges in Generations 2 to 4, and why validation accu-
racy in Generation 2 is temporarily lower with mentorship
(52.44%) than without mentorship (55.76%). The answer
lies in the interaction between NSGA-II non-dominated
sorting and the accuracy-monotonicity constraint in Eq.
(13). In Generation 1, 6 broad exploratory edges inject
soft-target regularization into the supernet, slightly slow-
ing pure cross-entropy fitting in Generation 2 (-3.32%).
By Generations 2 to 4, almost all 10 individuals enter the
3-objective Rank-0 Pareto front (|R>0| →0) or the few
dominated individuals already match the accuracy of the
low-FLOP Rank-0 members, causing Eq. (13) to automat-
ically suspend suboptimal distillation. Once the hierarchi-
cal phase consolidates elite high-accuracy leaders in Gen-
eration 4 (where EvoMentor-Net overtakes the control at
61.13% vs. 59.86%), 2 targeted elite mentorship edges ac-
tivate in Generation 5 and propel the final fine-tuned accu-
racy to 78.85% (+7.69% over the 71.16% control, yielding
an overall MEI = 0.0154 per generation).
3. Self-Regulating Curriculum Protection: This automatic
edge suspension in Generations 2 to 4 demonstrates that
Eq. (13) acts as a protective gate: rather than forcing dis-
tillation when no clearly superior teacher exists, the graph
withholds KD loss until accuracy-verified elite mentors
emerge.

5.2. Role of Accuracy Monotonicity in Multi-Objective Distil-
lation
A central mechanistic insight from Experiment 7 (Table 5) is
the necessity of the teacher-student accuracy-monotonicity con-
straint Accval(It) ≥Accval(Is) during multi-objective evolution-
ary search. In single-objective evolutionary algorithms, the top-
ranked individuals are by definition the most accurate models.
In multi-objective NSGA-II, however, the Rank-0 Pareto front
contains both high-accuracy models (e.g., 80.18% accuracy,
55.80 M FLOPs) and tiny, low-accuracy models (e.g., 38.98%
accuracy, 6.92 M FLOPs). Without filtering edges by valida-
tion accuracy, low-FLOP Pareto solutions degrade the popu-
lation via reverse distillation. Furthermore, when the popula-
tion temporarily lacks accuracy-superior teachers during mid-
evolutionary reorganization (Generations 2 to 4 in Table 5), sus-
pending distillation edges allows raw cross-entropy gradients
to separate the accuracy rankings before activating hierarchical
elite distillation in Generation 5 (+7.69% gain).
5.3. Computational Trade-offs
While tri-level co-evolution introduces additional forward
passes for mentor logit evaluation, three design choices keep
the total runtime of all eight experiments (including 5-seed sta-
tistical validation and cross-domain transfer) within 2 to 3 hours
on a single NVIDIA T4 GPU: (1) sampling Ksample = 3 students
per batch rather than all 10 individuals, (2) bounding mentor
in-degree at Kmax = 2 and caching detached teacher logits per
batch, and (3) evaluating fitness on a 1, 024-sample validation
subset with 4-batch BN recalibration during evolution while re-
serving full validation and 6-epoch standalone fine-tuning for
the discovered Pareto winner.
5.4. Limitations
To maintain rigorous scientific transparency, several limita-
tions of the present study are noted:
1. Spatial Resolution and Demographic Metadata Con-
straints in Histopathology: Both CIFAR-10 and PathM-
NIST were evaluated at an input resolution of 32 × 32
pixels, and PathMNIST was subsampled to 8,000 train-
ing and 2,000 validation patches to fit single-GPU multi-
experiment execution budgets.
As shown in Table 10,
downsampling colorectal tissue patches to 32 × 32 blurs
fine nuclear chromatin and fibrillar boundaries, contribut-
ing to the lower sensitivity observed in Stroma (Class
7, 63.25%) and Debris (Class 2, 65.62%).
In addi-
tion, because PathMNIST distributes anonymized tile-
level patches without patient-level sex or gender metadata,
sex- and gender-based subgroup analyses (SGBA) cannot
be performed on this cohort. Scaling EvoMentor-Net to
high-resolution whole-slide image patches (224 × 224 or
512×512) with patient-stratified demographic metadata is
required prior to clinical deployment.
2. Simulated Masking vs.
Physical Tensor Slicing: In
the current supernet implementation (Eq. 9), structured
channel pruning is enforced during forward propagation
by multiplying stage output tensors by a binary mask
m(s) ∈{0, 1}cs+1, and FLOP and parameter reductions are
computed analytically. While this models zeroed feature
channels during weight-sharing search, achieving physical
wall-clock inference speedup on edge hardware requires
exporting the final pruned sub-network into a standalone
sliced tensor graph with physically reduced convolutional
weight matrices.
3. Short
Evolution
Horizons
and
Differential
Fine-
Tuning: To execute all eight benchmark experiments and
5-seed runs within a single Kaggle T4 session, evolu-
tion was conducted over G ∈{3, 4, 5, 8} generations with
Ein = 2 inner epochs per generation. Additionally, in the
default protocol (run_evomentor), the standalone fine-
tuning budget scales with the mentorship mode (6 epochs
for evolved mentorship, 3 for static, and 2 for none).
While the generational tracking in E7 confirms that cur-
riculum mentorship outperforms unmentored training at
matched generation counts, equalizing post-search fine-
tuning epochs across all ablation branches over longer 50-
to-100-epoch schedules will further isolate pure distilla-
tion gains from fine-tuning duration.
4. Sample Size in Non-Parametric Statistical Tests: Al-
though the 5-seed paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test in
Experiment 6 achieves the maximum possible rank sum
(W = 15.00, p = 0.0312), n = 5 is the minimum sam-
ple size at which a one-sided Wilcoxon test can reach
p < 0.05. Expanding validation to 10 or more random
seeds across ImageNet-1K and full-scale MedMNIST v2
cohorts is a concrete direction for follow-up work.
6. Conclusion
This paper introduced EvoMentor-Net,
a tri-level co-
evolutionary framework that unifies macro-skeleton architec-
ture search, micro-cell DAG connectivity with structured chan-
nel pruning, and curriculum-driven self-distillation within a sin-
gle weight-sharing supernet.
By evolving a sparse directed
mentorship graph alongside architectural and pruning genomes,
and transitioning at 40% of the evolutionary budget from di-
verse exploration to accuracy-monotonic hierarchical refine-
ment, EvoMentor-Net mitigates weight-sharing gradient inter-
ference and prevents the negative transfer observed in static
