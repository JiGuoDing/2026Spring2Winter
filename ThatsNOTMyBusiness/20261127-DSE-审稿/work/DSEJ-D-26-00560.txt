Abstract:
Recommendation databases contain sensitive user preferences and interaction
records that support personalized services and statistical analysis. Although aggregate
query answering restricts direct access to individual records, successive exact
aggregate results may be combined to reconstruct confidential numerical values. This
study proposes an integrated privacy-preserving aggregate query answering
framework comprising three models: Simple Aggregate Query Answering (S-AQA),
History-Aware Aggregate Query Answering (H-AQA), and Incremental Utility-Aware
History-Based Aggregate Query Answering (IUH-AQA). S-AQA enforces a minimum-
support threshold, whereas H-AQA incorporates historical linear inference auditing to
prevent the exact reconstruction of designated protected values. IUH-AQA extends this
mechanism through incremental orthonormal row-space maintenance and utility-aware
query prioritization to improve computational efficiency and analytical usefulness while
preserving mandatory reconstruction-prevention constraints. Mathematical analysis
establishes the necessary and sufficient condition for exact linear reconstruction and
proves the equivalence of full-history and incremental auditing under identical historical
states and exact arithmetic. The framework is evaluated using MovieLens 32M and
Amazon Sales data, together with controlled reconstruction attacks, dataset-informed
workloads, and analytical scalability assessments. Across 800 controlled
reconstruction trials per dataset, S-AQA permitted target reconstruction in all trials,
whereas H-AQA and IUH-AQA prevented reconstruction in every evaluated case, with
query acceptance rates of 66.67\% compared with 100\% for S-AQA. In ablation
experiments, incremental auditing reduced mean auditing time by approximately 18-
fold and 14.1-fold for rating-like and price-like workloads, respectively. Utility-aware
prioritization also increased normalized weighted utility, although its greedy strategy
did not guarantee global optimality. The findings demonstrate that historical inference
auditing can preserve exact aggregate results while preventing specified cumulative
linear reconstruction attacks. The proposed framework provides a mathematical and
experimental foundation for integrating reconstruction resistance, incremental auditing,
and analytical utility in recommendation databases, subject to the stated threat-model
and experimental limitations.

behavioral attributes. Such information also supports statistical analysis [5–7], busi-

recommendation databases may expose confidential user information even when direct

1.1 Research Objectives
The primary objective of this study is to develop and evaluate an exact aggregate query
answering framework that integrates cumulative linear inference prevention, incremen-
tal auditing, and security-constrained analytical utility. The specific objectives are
to:
 Establish a controlled aggregate-only access architecture and a minimum-support
baseline for exact statistical result release.
 Develop a history-aware auditing mechanism to prevent exact reconstruction of
designated protected numerical coordinates from accumulated linear aggregate
constraints.
 Develop an incremental auditing mechanism that preserves the reconstruction-
prevention decisions of full-history analysis while reducing redundant computation.
 Integrate utility-aware query prioritization with mandatory disclosure restrictions
to improve the analytical value of approved queries.

ematical assumptions, computational requirements, and effects on analytical utility
differ substantially. For recommendation databases, these distinctions are particularly
important when analysts require exact statistical results derived from confidential
user preferences, ratings, and interaction histories. This section reviews relevant

2.1 Anonymization-Based Privacy Preservation
Anonymization-based techniques reduce identity and attribute disclosure risks by
transforming published datasets through generalization, suppression, or related mech-
anisms [26, 27]. Representative models include k-Anonymity [18], l-Diversity [28],
t-Closeness [29], km-Anonymity [30], and LKC-Privacy [31].
The k-Anonymity model requires each record to be indistinguishable from at
least k −1 other records with respect to selected quasi-identifiers. Although this
requirement reduces selected re-identification risks, it does not inherently prevent
sensitive-attribute disclosure when equivalence classes contain homogeneous values or
when adversaries possess relevant background knowledge. Moreover, extensive gen-
eralization and suppression may reduce data granularity and analytical accuracy
[26].
The l-Diversity model introduces diversity requirements for sensitive values within
equivalence classes. However, semantically similar values and skewed distributions may
still enable attribute inference. The t-Closeness model further constrains differences
between equivalence-class and overall sensitive-attribute distributions, although strict
distributional requirements may increase data transformation and reduce analytical
usefulness [27].
For transactional and set-valued datasets, km-Anonymity limits re-identification
risks arising from adversarial knowledge of item combinations, whereas LKC-Privacy
incorporates assumptions concerning background-knowledge size, record support, and
sensitive-attribute disclosure confidence. Their effectiveness depends on the specified
adversarial assumptions and may require transformations that affect transactional
patterns.
Although these models provide established foundations for privacy-preserving
data publication, their objectives differ from interactive aggregate query answer-
ing. In particular, anonymization does not independently guarantee that successive
exact aggregate results cannot be combined to reconstruct protected numerical val-
ues [23, 25]. Furthermore, minimum-support verification in aggregate query answering
should not be equated with k-Anonymity because the two mechanisms impose different
privacy requirements.
2.2 Anatomization-Based Privacy Preservation
Anatomization separates quasi-identifying attributes from sensitive attributes to
obscure their associations while preserving selected original values [19]. Related
approaches, including bucketization and slicing, partition records or attributes to
reduce disclosure risks while retaining useful statistical characteristics [32]. Com-
pared with extensive generalization, anatomization may preserve greater numerical
precision. Nevertheless, its effectiveness depends on partitioning strategies, residual
correlations, and adversarial background knowledge. In recommendation databases,
distinctive preferences and sparse interaction histories may facilitate linkage attacks.

More importantly, anatomization primarily regulates published data representations

queries. Consequently, it does not replace historical inference auditing.
2.3 Perturbation-Based Privacy Preservation
Perturbation-based techniques reduce disclosure risks by modifying original data or
released statistical results through noise addition, data swapping, numerical masking,
or related transformations [20]. These techniques provide flexibility in balancing pri-
vacy and analytical usefulness. However, numerical modification may distort aggregate
results, statistical distributions, and attribute relationships. Moreover, arbitrary per-
turbation does not establish a formal privacy guarantee without an explicitly defined
protection model. For applications requiring exact statistical results, perturbation may
therefore be unsuitable. Exact-result disclosure control adopts a different strategy by
restricting which aggregate results can be released rather than modifying their numer-
ical values. However, preserving numerical accuracy does not itself imply stronger
privacy protection.
2.4 Differential Privacy-Based Techniques
Differential Privacy provides formal probabilistic protection by limiting the influ-
ence of individual records on the distribution of released outputs [21]. Mechanisms
such as Laplace and Gaussian randomization introduce calibrated noise, while com-
position principles characterize privacy loss across repeated queries. These properties
provide substantial advantages for interactive statistical analysis. Nevertheless, ran-
domized outputs generally involve a privacy–accuracy trade-off, and repeated releases
require appropriate privacy-budget management. Consequently, conventional random-
ized mechanisms may not satisfy applications requiring every approved aggregate
result to equal the corresponding exact database statistic. Differential Privacy and
exact-result auditing therefore address different protection objectives. The former
provides probabilistic guarantees under a specified randomized mechanism, whereas
the latter selectively releases deterministic results according to a defined inference-
prevention criterion. The proposed framework does not claim equivalent privacy
guarantees.
2.5 Cryptography-Based Privacy Preservation
Cryptographic techniques protect confidential information during storage, trans-
mission, and computation [22]. Representative approaches include homomorphic
encryption, secure multiparty computation, and trusted execution environments.
These mechanisms support confidential computation across distributed or partially
trusted environments. However, protecting inputs and intermediate computations does
not necessarily prevent disclosure through final statistical outputs. An analyst may
still combine multiple authorized aggregate results to reconstruct protected numerical
values. Accordingly, cryptographic computation and historical inference auditing are
complementary. Cryptographic mechanisms protect selected aspects of computation,
whereas inference auditing evaluates the information disclosed by query results.

2.6 Statistical Disclosure Control and Aggregate Inference
Attacks
Statistical disclosure control regulates information released through analytical inter-
faces. Conventional approaches include minimum query-set sizes, output suppression,
and restrictions on overlapping query populations [23, 24]. Minimum-support verifica-
tion prevents selected small-population disclosures while preserving exact results for
eligible queries [23, 33, 34]. However, satisfying a population-size threshold does not
guarantee protection against cumulative inference. For example, consider two popula-
tions S1 and S2 = S1 \ {j}. Releasing their exact SUM results enables reconstruction
through xj = SUM(S1)−SUM(S2), even when both populations satisfy the minimum-
support requirement. Similarly, when the contributing non-null count m is known, the
relationship SUM(S) = m AVG(S) allows AVG results to be interpreted as equiva-
lent linear constraints. Nonlinear aggregates introduce additional risks. For example,
identical MIN and MAX results over the same nonempty population establish that
all contributing non-null values are equal. Such disclosure may occur regardless of
population size. These vulnerabilities demonstrate that minimum-support verification,
homogeneous-value disclosure prevention, and cumulative reconstruction control are
distinct requirements. In particular, linear inference auditing does not automatically
prevent nonlinear disclosure arising from MIN and MAX.
2.7 History-Based Inference Auditing
History-based inference auditing evaluates candidate queries against previously
released statistical information to identify cumulative disclosure risks [35–37]. Its
underlying principle is that individually admissible queries may collectively reveal
protected values. Early research established the importance of auditing overlapping
statistical queries and examined the computational challenges of inference control [23].
Subsequent work also investigated information leakage arising from both answered
and denied queries [24]. For exact linear aggregates, previously released results can
be represented as a consistent system Ax = b, where A contains query coefficient
vectors and b contains their corresponding exact results. Assuming that x ∈Rn is
otherwise unrestricted, a protected coordinate xj is uniquely reconstructable if and
only if ej ∈Row(A). This necessary and sufficient condition provides a mathemat-
ical foundation for evaluating whether an additional linear aggregate result would
enable exact reconstruction of a designated protected coordinate. Unlike independent
minimum-support verification, history-based auditing accounts for the accumulated
information represented by previously released constraints. Nevertheless, the guaran-
tee applies only under its specified assumptions. Nonlinear aggregates, bounded or
discrete domains, auxiliary knowledge, and observable query decisions may introduce
additional disclosure channels [24, 25]. Therefore, linear inference auditing should not
be interpreted as comprehensive privacy protection.

2.8 Incremental Inference Auditing and Computational
Efficiency
The computational efficiency of history-based auditing depends on how accumulated
constraints are represented and maintained. Repeatedly constructing and analyzing
the complete historical matrix may introduce redundant computation as the number
of released queries increases [23, 25]. Incremental linear-algebra techniques address
this limitation by maintaining reusable representations of accumulated constraints
[25, 38, 39]. For exact linear reconstruction auditing, the relevant information is the row
space of the released constraint matrix. An incremental mechanism can maintain an
orthonormal basis Bt satisfying Row(Bt) = Row(At). When a newly approved query
introduces an independent constraint, the basis can be updated without reconstruct-
ing the complete historical matrix. Dependent queries do not increase the dimension of
the accumulated row space. However, incremental basis maintenance is an established
mathematical technique rather than an independently novel contribution. Yang et al.
[25], for example, investigated normalized query basis matrices and auditing mech-
anisms for aggregate database queries. Furthermore, the computational advantages
of incremental auditing depend on constraint rank, data dimensionality, query spar-
sity, history length, and numerical stability. Therefore, formal complexity analysis and
experimental comparisons are required to determine when incremental maintenance
improves practical efficiency.
2.9 Security-Constrained Analytical Utility
Privacy-preserving aggregate query answering must balance mandatory disclosure
restrictions with the analytical usefulness of released information. For randomized
mechanisms, utility is frequently associated with numerical accuracy [40]. In exact-
result systems, however, approved outputs retain their original numerical values;
analytical utility therefore depends primarily on query availability, selection, and rela-
tive importance. A mechanism that rejects all queries may prevent disclosure through
released results but provides little analytical value. Conversely, unrestricted query
answering may enable cumulative reconstruction. Moreover, query-processing order
influences future admissibility [41]. Releasing one constraint may prevent another,
potentially more valuable, query from being safely answered. Consequently, conven-
tional first-come, first-served processing does not necessarily maximize the analytical
value of approved queries. Security-constrained utility-aware selection addresses this
limitation by assigning analytical importance to candidate queries while preserving
mandatory disclosure restrictions. Its effectiveness depends on the utility model, query
workload, processing strategy, and security constraints. Accordingly, utility-aware
mechanisms should be evaluated against sequential auditing and other appropriate
baselines using query availability and weighted analytical utility rather than approval
counts alone.

3.1.6 Threat Model and Security Assumptions
The framework considers an analytical adversary who can submit authorized aggre-
gate queries, observe released results, retain previous responses, and adapt subsequent
queries. The adversary may know query predicates and contributing populations but
cannot directly access protected values or compromise the trusted query processor.
The formal security analysis assumes:
 A fixed protected vector throughout the audited history.
 Otherwise unrestricted real-valued protected coordinates.
 Complete accounting of applicable previously released linear constraints.
 A trusted query processor and a secure initial historical state.
 Exact arithmetic for formal reconstruction decisions.
Under these assumptions, historical auditing prevents additional linear aggregate
releases that would uniquely reconstruct designated protected coordinates. The guar-
antee does not automatically address nonlinear inference, bounded or discrete domains,
arbitrary auxiliary knowledge, or leakage through observable query decisions. Prac-
tical floating-point implementations also require conservative numerical handling or
certified procedures to preserve the formal security criterion.

5.1 Experimental Setup

the preceding sections and assesses the practical implications of minimum-support
verification, historical inference auditing, incremental row-space maintenance, and
utility-aware query prioritization. Particular emphasis is placed on distinguishing the
reconstruction-prevention guarantees of H-AQA and IUH-AQA from the support-

database configurations, candidate query workloads, and applicable security policies.

5.2 Results and Discussion
The experimental evaluation investigates the performance of S-AQA, H-AQA, and
IUH-AQA in terms of reconstruction resistance, auditing correctness, computational
efficiency, numerical accuracy, exact-answer availability, and analytical utility. Dif-
ferential Privacy and k-Anonymity are included as conceptually distinct privacy
baselines; their guarantees are not equated with the linear reconstruction criterion.
The results are organized according to the distinct objectives of each experiment.
Particular attention is given to separating theoretically established reconstruction-
prevention properties from empirical observations and distinguishing the contributions
of historical auditing, incremental constraint maintenance, and utility-aware query
prioritization.

exact optimization computationally manageable and to enable direct comparisons with
a globally optimal reference. Consequently, the reported optimality gaps should not be
generalized to the full MovieLens 32M or Amazon Sales datasets without additional
experiments involving larger protected-coordinate sets, more diverse query predicates,
and broader workload distributions.
Overall, this experiment demonstrates that utility-aware prioritization can sub-
stantially improve weighted analytical utility while preserving the exact linear
reconstruction-prevention criterion. The comparison with globally optimal selection
further clarifies the trade-off between computationally efficient greedy query order-
ing and optimal subset selection, providing empirical support for the utility-oriented
design of IUH-AQA under the evaluated dataset-derived workloads.

5.3 Summary of Experimental Findings
The controlled reconstruction experiments demonstrate that minimum-support veri-
fication alone is insufficient to prevent exact inference from overlapping SUM queries.
Across 800 attack-oriented trials per dataset, S-AQA permitted reconstruction of the
designated target in every trial, whereas H-AQA and IUH-AQA prevented reconstruc-
tion in the evaluated cases. The corresponding query acceptance rates were 100% and
66.67%, respectively. These outcomes characterize deliberately constructed attacks,
not general-purpose query workloads. The formal analysis establishes that H-AQA
and IUH-AQA enforce equivalent linear reconstruction criteria when applied to the
same query sequence and historical state. Analytical memory estimates suggest that
incremental row-space maintenance is particularly economical when many released
constraints are linearly dependent. The preliminary timing profiles, Differential Pri-
vacy accuracy scenarios, threshold-sensitivity curves, and weighted-utility trends
remain illustrative or analytically derived where independent execution measure-
ments are unavailable; they should not be presented as verified empirical performance

6 Conclusions
This study presented an integrated privacy-preserving aggregate query answering
framework for recommendation databases, addressing the challenge of releasing exact
statistical results without enabling cumulative reconstruction of designated confiden-
tial numerical values. Three complementary models were developed and evaluated:
S-AQA, H-AQA, and IUH-AQA. S-AQA provides a lightweight minimum-support
mechanism, H-AQA incorporates historical linear inference auditing, and IUH-AQA
combines incremental row-space maintenance with utility-aware query prioritization.
Together, these models establish a unified framework for examining the relationships
among exact-answer accuracy, reconstruction resistance, computational efficiency, and
analytical utility.
The mathematical analysis established that a protected numerical coordinate is
uniquely reconstructable from a consistent system of exact linear aggregate constraints
if and only if its corresponding standard basis vector belongs to the row space of
the accumulated constraint matrix. Based on this criterion, H-AQA prevents the
release of additional linear constraints that would uniquely determine protected coor-
dinates. IUH-AQA preserves the same reconstruction-prevention condition through an
incrementally maintained orthonormal basis. The formal results further establish the
equivalence of full-history and incremental auditing under exact arithmetic, identical
candidate queries, and equivalent historical states. These properties provide a rigorous
foundation for preventing cumulative exact linear reconstruction without perturbing
approved aggregate results.
The controlled reconstruction experiments demonstrated the limitations of inde-
pendent minimum-support verification. Across 800 attack-oriented trials for each
dataset, S-AQA permitted reconstruction of the designated protected value in every
trial, despite satisfying the minimum-support requirements. In contrast, H-AQA and
IUH-AQA prevented target reconstruction in all evaluated cases while approving
66.67% of submitted queries. These findings confirm that historical inference auditing
provides protection against the evaluated cumulative reconstruction attacks, although
the observed acceptance rates reflect deliberately constructed adversarial workloads
rather than general database-query distributions.
The computational evaluation identified potential efficiency advantages associ-
ated with incremental auditing. In dataset-informed ablation experiments, IUH-AQA
reduced mean auditing time by approximately 18-fold for the MovieLens-like workload
and 14.1-fold for the Amazon-like workload relative to full-history auditing. Analyti-
cal storage estimates further indicated that incremental row-space representations can
reduce active auditing-state memory when accumulated constraints exhibit substantial

Utility-aware query prioritization provided an additional mechanism for improv-

criterion. Its formal guarantees are restricted to consistent linear constraints over
otherwise unrestricted real-valued protected variables, assuming a secure initial his-

Nonlinear inference, bounded or discrete attribute domains, auxiliary knowledge,
observable query decisions, and floating-point numerical instability are not compre-
hensively addressed by these guarantees. Moreover, several scalability and comparative
privacy analyses remain illustrative or analytically derived, requiring additional repro-
ducible implementation measurements before broader empirical conclusions can be
established.
Overall, the findings demonstrate that cumulative inference auditing is essential
when exact aggregate results are released from confidential recommendation databases.
By integrating historical reconstruction prevention, incremental constraint mainte-
nance, and security-constrained utility-aware prioritization, IUH-AQA provides a
coherent approach to balancing disclosure control, computational requirements, and
analytical usefulness. The principal contribution lies in the formal integration and com-
parative characterization of these mechanisms, providing a foundation for controlled
exact aggregate query answering under explicitly defined privacy assumptions.
